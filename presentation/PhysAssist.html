The hardest part turned out to be neither the search nor the language model, but knowing whether any of it is right.

The problem

Two tools, two failure modes

Ask a general chatbot

It answers fluently. Some of the answer will be wrong, and nothing on the screen tells you which part.

Ask a search engine

You get pages, not an answer. You still have to read them.

In a field where being confidently wrong about a nulling time is a clinical problem, traceability is not optional.
The idea

A librarian and a writer, working as a pair

The librarian

Searches a fixed shelf of trusted documents and pulls out the handful most likely to contain the answer.

The writer

A language model that reads only those passages and composes a reply, citing which passage each claim came from.

If the librarian brings the wrong pages, the answer is wrong no matter how good the writer is.

Almost every design decision in this project follows from that one sentence. The writer holds no MRI knowledge we rely on — the only material in front of it is six passages, each with a real URL a reader can open and check.

How RAG works

First, the knowledge is cut into chunks

A chunk must carry its own meaning

  • Keep a question with its answer.
  • Keep an equation with the lines that define its symbols.
  • Drop navigation, reference lists and problem sets — they match questions and answer nothing.
  • A fact split across two chunks is findable from neither.

Chunking looks like preprocessing. It is where most of the retrieval quality is won or lost — which is why it took three generations (next section).

How RAG works

A question takes the same path to the writer

  1. Embed the question with the same model that embedded the chunks, so both live in one space.
  2. Find the nearest chunks by cosine similarity — 60 candidates, re-scored by a cross-encoder, best six kept.
  3. Hand only those six to the writer. It composes the answer from them, citing [1], [2] against each passage.
  4. Show the sources. The reader can open every page the answer rests on.

Retrieval is search by meaning rather than by keyword; generation is writing under constraint. The next slide shows the actual pipeline.

The gap is scanned papers

Roughly 155 arrived as images and went through OCR — which silently destroys the subscripts that carry the meaning. B0 becomes B0, sometimes B0B. Those pages got a separate correction pass.

Why not fine-tune a model on all this?

Because then you cannot see where an answer came from. Training text into weights gives fluency and no traceability. Keeping it on a shelf is slower — and every sentence gets a URL behind it.

Generation 3

Physics-aware

For papers and books. Never split a math block from its definitions: TI = T1 · ln 2 travels with the lines that say what TI and T1 are. Heading path prefixed; problem sets skipped.

Each generation fixed a failure the previous one created — and the middle one is where two thirds of the junk left the index.

Chunking · the subtlest bug

The fix was an ordering change, not a code change

The plan: strip boilerplate, then cut the reference tail off at the References heading.

But References appears on 587 pages — which makes it boilerplate by the stripper's own definition. The stripper deleted the marker before the tail-cutter could find it, so the tails stayed in.

Cut the tail first, then strip boilerplate.

Reference-polluted chunks fell from 696 to 72, and the index went from 74,613 to 50,454 chunks — a third of it was never content.

Guessing wrong does not degrade the answer slightly — it retrieves the wrong literature entirely. A reader asking about FA in diffusion and one asking about FA as a sequence parameter want disjoint sets of pages.

Retrieval · three more ways search fails

Meaning, not word-matching

Same word, different quantity

"Bandwidth" may be receiver or transmit; "frequency" may be Larmor or an offset. Encoded as a readable table of 14 scenarios keyed on trigger phrases — rules an expert can correct, not weights buried in a model.

No shared words at all

"Hydrogen resonates at 64 MHz at 1.5 T — what is the frequency at 7 T?" The answering page explains the Larmor equation. "Larmor" appears nowhere in the question. The system must know the concept, not the word.

Long papers crowd out short pages

A paper matches many queries moderately; a Q&A page matches one exactly. On the questions we got wrong, 34 of 75 top-three slots were papers when a site page was better. Current fix: a hand-set preference for site pages — a tuned constant not yet justified by experiment.

The writer

A language model on a short leash

Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, quantised (AWQ) and served locally with vLLM on a single RTX PRO 5000. No question, passage, or answer leaves the machine — for a corpus of copyrighted material, the only defensible choice.

Its standing instructions

  1. Lead with the answer.
  2. Cite inline as [1], [2] against the numbered passages.
  3. Give the equation when one is relevant.
  4. Correct a false premise before answering it.
  5. Mark outside knowledge — established physics may be used, flagged as such, never to invent the meaning of a term.

The fifth rule was earned

A stricter version forbade anything outside the retrieved passages. The model responded by hedging — "the exact value is not specified in the provided context" — on questions it could have answered.

Measured on 55 calculation questions. Refusing to commit was costing more than being wrong.

The application

What a user actually sees

A locally served chat interface. The answer streams back with equations typeset and every claim marked with its passage. Below it: the source list — title, site or paper, retrieval score, and a live link.

Three details that matter

  • Sources are shown, not summarised. An answer whose citation does not support it is visibly wrong — the entire safety argument.
  • Follow-ups refine rather than restart. "Shorter" or "just the equation" edits the previous answer instead of re-searching.
  • Every answer can be rated, stored with the question, the answer, and the exact passages retrieved.

The feedback loop is the point

A thumbs-down is a labelled retrieval failure: what was asked and what the librarian brought back — precisely the diagnostic pair. A review tool groups negatives by topic to show where the shelf is thin.

Question → rating → the gap it reveals → new documents → re-index

The only mechanism that improves the system from use rather than an engineer's guess. So far almost entirely unused — one rating exists.

Two gaps only a person can close

Where the machine runs out

Both remaining gaps have the same shape: a question about MRI that no computation answers, because the answer is a matter of domain fact. Review pages for all four are built and waiting — click a verdict per item, export a file.

Evaluation

The hardest part was not building it

Two independent measures

Did the librarian find the right page? Scored against questions where the site's own author linked the answer.

Does the writer agree with the expert? 735 question–answer pairs, split into train, development, and held-out test.

Every comparison runs through a resampling test

Shuffle the data twenty thousand times and ask whether an improvement this large could be luck. This threw out three changes that felt like clear wins — including a hybrid keyword-plus-semantic search that looked convincingly better and was noise.

Building the pipeline took a fraction of the effort. Establishing whether any change was an improvement took the rest.

Known weaknesses, stated plainly

  • The system cannot say "I don't know." Unanswerable questions still get an answer; abstention is unbuilt.
  • The automatic grader agrees with itself only about two thirds of the time, so small measured differences cannot be trusted.
  • Retrieval confidence predicts nothing — low-scoring retrievals are as often correct as high-scoring ones.
  • One question harvester is still broken: it glues multiple-choice options onto stems and destroys subscripts.

Nothing about the system changed. The ruler changed.

Judging a pooled set of 1,776 question–document pairs showed a median of eight genuinely relevant pages per question. That reframes eleven experiments with no improvement: not "we keep failing to find the trick" but "there was very little left to find."

Every headline number has moved when measured more carefully — always toward less than claimed. A result saying everything is nearly perfect deserves more suspicion, not less. Twenty expert judgements decide whether it survives.

What happens next

In order

  1. Twenty relevance judgements from a physicist. Everything else waits behind this — it determines whether the retrieval work so far was warranted or we have been reading a bent ruler.
  2. Validate the test questions and the acronym defaults. Until these exist, further experiments only produce numbers nobody should act on.
  3. Put the app in front of real users and collect ratings. Fifty disappointed answers would tell us more about where the shelf is thin than any offline metric.
  4. Teach the system to decline. An honest "the corpus does not cover this" is worth more in clinical physics than a confident paragraph.
  5. Grow the shelf one source at a time, measuring after each — so that when something improves, we know what did it.
Looking further ahead

Next: a conversation that reasons

Today

One question in, one cited answer out. Follow-ups such as "shorter" edit the previous answer, but the system does not carry a line of reasoning from turn to turn.

Planned

A reasoning ("chain-of-thought") model as the writer: it works through the problem step by step — identifying the relevant physics, choosing the equation, checking units — before committing to an answer, and it can hold a multi-turn conversation in which the user questions, corrects, and refines.

What does not change

  • The librarian still decides what the writer sees; reasoning happens over retrieved passages, not over memory.
  • Every step and every claim stays traceable to a page the user can open.
  • Everything runs locally — the conversation never leaves the workstation.
A model that can show its working is easier to catch when it is wrong — which, in clinical physics, is the whole point.
